Auction escrow and trust account questions, answered in plain words

If your auction house, auction company or estate-sale business holds buyer payments and consignor proceeds in a trust or escrow account, you have to be able to show, every month, that the money in the bank matches what you owe each consignor. What an auction trust or escrow account is, and whose money is in it

What is an auction escrow account, and whose money is in it?

An auction escrow account (many states call it a trust account, client account or custodial account) is a separate bank account that holds money belonging to other people: buyer payments waiting to be settled and consignor proceeds waiting to be paid out. Only your earned commission and agreed fees ever belong to the business. Louisiana's board describes it as money you hold for a third party, the same way a realtor or attorney holds client funds.

Do auctioneers have to keep consignor money in a separate account?

In most states that license auctioneers, yes. Texas (16 TAC §67.70) requires proceeds belonging to others to go into a trust or escrow account unless the consignor is paid at the sale, and Illinois (68 Ill. Admin. Code Pt. 1440) bars mixing money held for others with personal or business accounts. Some states waive the separate-account rule if consignors are paid within a few days of the sale, and estate-sale companies may fall under different rules than licensed auctioneers, so check your own state board and your consignment contract.

What does commingling mean, and can I deposit auction money into my operating account first?

Commingling means mixing money you hold for consignors with the business's own money, such as depositing buyer payments into operating checking, paying rent or license fees from the escrow account, or using one sale's receipts to pay another consignor. State boards treat it as a serious trust-account violation. Deposit auction proceeds into the trust account, pay consignors from it, and move your commission out only against the settlement that earned it.

How to do the monthly trust account reconciliation

What is a three-way reconciliation for an auction trust account?

It is a comparison of three figures for the same date: (1) the bank statement balance adjusted for deposits in transit and outstanding checks, (2) the trust balance in your own books, and (3) the total you still owe each consignor and buyer. When all three agree, the account is in balance; any difference needs a named explanation. The monthly reconciliation guide walks through the routine step by step.

How is a trust account reconciliation different from a bank reconciliation in QuickBooks?

A QuickBooks bank reconciliation proves your books match the bank, which covers figures 1 and 2 above. It does not prove that the money in the account is fully owed to identifiable consignors. That third comparison, against the consignor ledger, is what makes it a trust reconciliation, and it is the part an examiner cares about.

How often do I have to reconcile the escrow account?

It depends on the state, but monthly is the common standard. Wisconsin (SPS 125.12) requires a written reconciliation of the trust account each month and a check register with date, payee, check number and amount, and Florida's board guidance also asks for a monthly reconciliation with a signed, dated record. Even where no schedule is written down, many boards can examine the account at any time, so a monthly reconciliation done while the records are fresh is the practical minimum.

What records do I need to reconcile an auction trust account each month?

You need three sets of records for the same period: the settlement report from your auction software for every auction with activity, the record of consignor payouts with dates and check numbers from your accounting system, and the trust account bank statement. You also need last month's reconciled ending balance as your opening balance. The export guide lists the Auction Flex, QuickBooks/IIF and bank statement files SaleLedger reads.

How do I calculate the adjusted bank balance for a trust account?

Start with the statement ending balance, add deposits in transit (money you received and recorded that the bank has not shown yet), and subtract outstanding checks (consignor checks you wrote that have not cleared). For example, a $48,200 statement plus a $3,100 deposit in transit, less $6,300 in outstanding checks, gives an adjusted balance of $45,000. If the same item is still outstanding next month, look into it rather than carrying it forward forever.

How do I work out how much I still owe each consignor?

For each consignor, take what you owed at the start of the period, add their net proceeds from settlements (sales less commission and agreed fees), and subtract what you paid them. The sum across all consignors, plus any buyer credits held, is the consignor ledger or subledger total. Match consignors by the exact name used in both your auction software and your accounting file, because one consignor spelled two ways is a common reason a payout seems to disappear.

Is there a trust account reconciliation template or spreadsheet for auction companies?

Yes, a spreadsheet works for a small operation if it has four tabs: receipts, disbursements, a consignor balance tab and a summary that computes the three balances with formulas rather than typed numbers. Why the trust account doesn't balance, and what to do

Why doesn't my trust account balance match what I owe consignors?

The usual causes are a consignor paid more or less than the settlement, a deposit with no auction or buyer behind it, a transfer to operating with no settlement to support it, a bank fee taken from trust, an auction whose settlements were left out of the period, or an old uncashed check. Each one has its own fix, from recovering an overpayment to adding the missing settlement report. The exception walk-through shows how to trace one, and the exception table lists the common ones.

My bank and ledger agree but the consignor total is off. What does that mean?

It means the cash is accounted for but your records disagree about who owns it. Either a consignor was over- or underpaid, a settlement was never entered, or money is sitting in the account with no identified owner. For example, if a $1,200 settlement was never recorded, the consignor total comes out $1,200 short of the ledger, and that gap points you to the missing settlement. The consignor fund reconciliation page works through this case.

Can I just post an adjusting entry to make the reconciliation balance?

No. A plug entry that forces the numbers to agree hides the very difference an examiner or consignor will ask about. Record the month as out of balance, write down the difference and what you checked, and resolve it next month. An open item with a written explanation is defensible; an unexplained adjustment is not.

Can bank service charges be paid out of the escrow account?

Fees should not come out of consignor money. Illinois allows a licensee to put their own funds in the escrow account only to open it or to keep enough balance to avoid service charges, and Wisconsin allows funds to cover service charges on the trust account. If a fee is taken from trust, reimburse it from operating and record it, and consider asking the bank to waive fees on the trust account.

Can my commission stay in the trust account, and when should I move it?

Earned commission belongs to the business, so it should move to operating promptly and be documented against the settlement that earned it. Leaving it in trust makes the consignor total harder to prove, and moving it with no settlement to point to looks like an unsupported withdrawal. If commission is still in the account at month-end, show it as its own line so it is visible in the reconciliation.

What do I do about an uncashed consignor check or a consignor I can't reach?

List it as an outstanding check while it is recent, then contact the consignor and reissue if needed. If the money stays unclaimed, state unclaimed-property (escheat) laws usually decide what happens next, generally based on the payee's last known state, so check that state's dormancy period and reporting rules. Do not net a stale balance against other consignors or move it to operating.

Deadlines, record keeping and state board audits

How long do I have to pay a consignor after an auction?

It depends on the state and your contract. Texas (16 TAC §67.70) generally requires payment within 15 banking days after the auction unless the written contract says otherwise, and Louisiana (R.S. 37:3125) requires payment within 30 days of receiving the funds or 60 days from the sale, whichever is less. A consignment agreement can set a shorter deadline, and you are bound by it.

How quickly must auction proceeds go into the escrow account?

Some states set a short deposit deadline separate from the payout deadline. Texas requires proceeds belonging to others to be deposited in a trust or escrow account within 72 hours of the auction unless the consignor is paid at the sale. Check your state rule, because a deposit deadline and a consignor payment deadline are two different requirements.

How long do I need to keep trust account records?

Texas sets a two-year minimum for itemized auction records, and other states name three or five years. Keep the monthly reconciliation report, the check register, the settlement reports, bank statements and deposit records together, so any month can be reproduced from the evidence. Keep the whole set for the longest period any rule that applies to you names.

What happens if the state auctioneer board audits my escrow account?

Most states do not require a periodic trust account filing; instead the board can examine the account and your records, often after a complaint. Examiners typically ask for reconciliations, the check register and the records behind each deposit and payout, and boards can discipline licensees for trust-account violations such as late payment, commingling or missing records. Being able to hand over a reconciliation for every month, not just the latest one, is the strongest position. Who gets the interest on an auction escrow account?

State rules differ. Wisconsin allows trust funds in an interest-bearing account but none of the interest may benefit the auctioneer, and Ohio (ORC 4707.024) requires records of deposits, withdrawals and interest accrued in the client trust account. Check your state rule and your consignment contract before keeping any interest.

Do I have to file IRS Form 8300 when a buyer pays more than $10,000 in cash?

Generally, yes. A business that receives more than $10,000 in cash in one transaction or related transactions must file Form 8300 with the IRS within 15 days. That is a federal cash-reporting duty separate from your state trust account rules; talk to your CPA about how it applies to your sales.
